\subsection{Research question}
\review{Are people more likely to treat a robot partner as part of their team when the score is shared rather than split, and does this hold regardless of how well the robot performs?}

\subsection{Related work}
\begin{reviewblock}
Research on human teams suggests that how outcomes are shared shapes how people see their partners. Deutsch's (1949) theory of cooperation and competition holds that people who share a goal, so that one person's success helps the other, treat each other as allies, while people whose goals conflict treat each other as rivals. How rewards are divided also matters (Deutsch, 1975): an equal split tends to support solidarity and team harmony, while dividing by individual contribution (equity) puts the focus on each person's own output. Whether individual contributions are visible also changes behaviour in groups: people put in less effort when their own contribution cannot be identified, a pattern known as social loafing (Karau \& Williams, 1993). Together, these suggest that showing one shared score, rather than each player's score, could change how a partner is perceived.

Similar effects appear with computers and robots. Nass, Fogg and Moon (1996) found that people treated a computer as a teammate, rating it as more similar and more cooperative, when they shared a goal and an outcome with it. Robots presented as members of a participant's own group are anthropomorphised more and rated more positively (Kuchenbrandt et al., 2013), and can even be favoured over humans from another group (Fraune, \v{S}abanovi\'c \& Smith, 2017). Closest to our study, Bennett et al.\ (2025) compared collaborative and competitive teams in a computerised game modelled on an arcade classic, and found that the benefit of collaborating seen in human pairs was much smaller when the partner was a machine. None of these studies varies how the score is displayed to a person playing with a robot partner, or tests whether the effect of a shared score survives the robot performing badly, which is what our study addresses.
\end{reviewblock}

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\paragraph{H1:} \review{Participants will rate the robot as more of a teammate when the score is shared than when it is split.}

\paragraph{H2:} \review{This effect of a shared score will hold whatever the robot's performance: at both the good and the bad performance level, participants who see a shared score will rate the robot as more of a teammate than those who see split scores.}
