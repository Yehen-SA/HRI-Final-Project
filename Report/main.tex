\section{Research Question and Hypotheses}
\spec{Choice of Question and Hypothesis (15 pts)}

\subsection{Initial ideas}
\begin{reviewblock}
\textbf{Chosen direction:} shared vs split score, with the robot's performance varied:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Score structure: shared score vs split (per-player) score
    \item Robot performance: worse than, matching, or better than the participant
    \item Blame attribution was considered but left out to keep the study simple; it is possible future work
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Game:} co-operative Atari Breakout. The participant and robot each control a paddle and defend shared lives; each brick is credited to the last paddle to hit the ball.

\textbf{Dependent variables considered:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Whether the participant sees the robot as a teammate, competitor or tool
    \item Reward allocation: how the participant splits bonus points between themselves and the robot
    \item Peer review of the robot as a team member
    \item The participant's estimate of how much the robot contributed, compared with its actual contribution
    \item Likeability of the robot
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Related work:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Human teams: cooperation vs competition (Deutsch 1949), equality vs equity in reward splitting (Deutsch 1975), social loafing (Karau \& Williams 1993)
    \item Humans with computers and robots: ``Can computers be teammates?'' (Nass et al.\ 1996), in-group robots (Kuchenbrandt et al.\ 2013; Fraune et al.\ 2017)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Other design options:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Score structure varied between subjects and robot performance within subjects \fix{confirm with group}
    \item The robot presented as a different robot each round, with a new name and colour
    \item The robot's performance controlled by adapting it to the participant's score
\end{itemize}
\end{reviewblock}

\subsection{Research question}
Are people more likely to treat a robot partner as part of their team when the score is shared rather than split, and does this depend on how well the robot performs relative to them?

\subsection{Hypotheses}
We hypothesise that participants who see a shared score will treat the robot more as a teammate than participants who see a split score, and that a worse-performing robot will feel less like a teammate than a better-performing robot under both the shared and split scores.
