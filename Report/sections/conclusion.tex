\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

\subsection{Data analysis}
Teammate perception was scored as the mean of ``part of my team'' and ``common goal''. Likeability was the mean of the five Godspeed items, and steal and rescue rates are a share of the chances the participant had. A participant's two rounds are not independent, so every test uses one value per participant and compares the Shared and Split groups with Welch's $t$-test, which allows for unequal group sizes:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{H1}: each participant's mean over both rounds. $H_0$: the groups do not differ; $H_A$: they differ.
    \item \textit{H2}: each participant's Good$-$Bad difference. $H_0$: the Shared--Split difference is the same for both robots; $H_A$: it differs.
\end{itemize}
The hypotheses are tested on teammate perception; the other measures are secondary and tested the same way. Unless stated, $p$-values below are those in Table~\ref{tab:results}.

\subsection{Evaluation}
The manipulation worked: the good robot was rated more capable than the bad one (mean rating 3.82 vs 2.05 out of 5; paired $t$-test on each participant's Good$-$Bad difference in rating, $p<.001$).

\textbf{H1 was not supported.} Teammate perception did not differ between the Shared and Split groups (3.30 vs 3.21, the group means of each participant's average over both rounds; Welch's $t$-test, H1 column, $p=.80$), so $H_0$ was not rejected.

\textbf{H2 could not be supported.} The Shared--Split difference did not change between the good and bad robots (0.90 vs 0.33, the group means of each participant's Good$-$Bad difference; Welch's $t$-test, H2 column, $p=.45$), so $H_0$ was not rejected. This is consistent with H2, but with no effect in H1 there was no effect to hold.

The secondary measures agreed: likeability, steal rate and rescue rates did not differ between the groups (H1 column, all $p\geq.31$). The one value below .05 is prompted rescues in the H2 column ($p=.049$): Shared participants answered more of the bad robot's help requests, and Split participants more of the good robot's. As a 0--2 count and one of twelve tests, we treat it as a lead for future work, not a finding.

Shared-score participants' estimates of the robot's share of the points did not differ significantly from its real share (good robot: 46.0\% estimated vs 36.7\% actual, $p=.17$; bad robot: 28.5\% vs 26.7\%, $p=.79$; paired $t$-tests on each participant's estimate and actual share, $n=10$), so a single team score did not hide how well the robot played.

Overall, the robot's skill and behaviour, not the scoreboard, shaped how participants saw it. Its speech and help requests may already have framed it as a teammate in both conditions, leaving little room for the score display to matter. This fits Bennett et al.\ (2025), who found weaker team effects with a machine partner.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{Sample size}: with 10 and 12 participants per group, only very large effects could be detected, and participants were mostly young adults recruited by word of mouth.
    \item \textit{Measures}: the teammate score rests on two questionnaire items, and participants had almost no chances (0--5) to steal from the bad robot, so those steal rates are unreliable.
    \item \textit{Short, unsupervised sessions}: two 2-minute rounds played online may be too short for a sense of team to form, and we could not check that participants watched the score.
    \item \textit{Order}: robot names were tied to the round.
\end{itemize}
Future work should vary factors we held fixed:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{Focus on the score}: a game where the score is more crucial to the gameplay, for example one with several levels that each need a score threshold to progress, so that participants pay attention to how the score is shown.
    \item \textit{Robot speech and behaviour}: robots with different speech and behaviour, including one that does not speak or ask for help, to separate the effect of the score display from the robot's social behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}
We tested whether showing a shared team score, rather than separate scores, makes people see a robot partner as a teammate, at two levels of robot performance. Participants clearly noticed the difference between the good and bad robots, but the score display did not change teammate perception or in-game behaviour at either level. In this short cooperative game, how the robot played and behaved mattered more than how the score was shown.
